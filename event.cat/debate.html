Debate

become the master debater

By Alivia Gomez

Captain 2025-26
Public Forum Debate is a debate category characterized by its two-person nature and its reliance on impromptu argumentation rather than strictly evidence. Every month, a new topic gets voted on by coaches, giving students roughly 3 weeks to research and collaborate with their partner to build two cases: an affirmation (pro) and a negation (con). These cases are structured around the “resolution”, AKA, a firm decision to do or not do something. In the past, resolutions have varied across both domestic and international issues, whether that be nuclear energy, international criminal courts, executive orders, or even sports gambling. The burden of the affirmation is one of proof. In other words, you have the responsibility of proving that the resolution is something beneficial to the status quo, which is what the negation argues for. You will not know which case you’re debating until you get into the room and do a coin flip. Whoever wins gets to decide the speaking order or which case they get to argue. Whoever lost the coin flip gets to choose whichever option is leftover. For example, if I win a coin toss and decide I want to have the last word with the judge, I can pick the second speaker. This then leaves you, my opponent, with the decision of if you want to be aff/neg. After sides have been chosen, you will begin the debate. The order: 1st speaker Constructive (4 mins): this speech lays out your “contentions”, AKA, your reasons for agreeing (pro) or disagreeing (neg) with the resolution. For example, if the resolution states that executive orders are on balance, more beneficial than harmful, as affirmation my contentions may be: 1. Executive orders are efficient in times of emergency and 2. They streamline agencies. For this speech, you will usually have 2-3 contentions. That is a choice you will make with your partner based on the resolution of the month. This speech is pre-written and usually begins with a short introduction. Additionally, it’s often important to outline definitions (as many judges are from the community and are not familiar with PF debate) and outline your “framework”, AKA, your basis for why your argument is superior. So my framework may be: the winner of this debate should be the side that proves better outcomes for the American people. This framework is what the judge will reflect on when deciding who will win. 2nd speaker Constructure (4 mins): This speech is the exact same as the previous, however it is the other teams turn. Speaking second in the debate is often an advantage as you have longer to prepare for rebutting arguments and prepare questions. 1st Crossfire (3 mins): The two “1st speakers” from each team will now engage in crossfire. Typically, whoever spoke first in the debate gets the first question. From then on, its fair game, but most of the time you will follow a “back and forth” structure of asking and answering questions. This is where your speaking shines most, as you can show how quick you can defend your case on the spot. Avoid using “Did you know…” or “Are you aware of…” arguments that are just an excuse to read evidence. This is a time to attack, clarify, and clash. 1st Rebuttal/Deconstruction speech (4 mins): This speech is now the first time the “2nd speaker” within your own team will speak. The best structure of deconstructing an argument is typically beginning with a short intro, going over your “roadmap” (how your deconstruction will play out) and then getting into your opponents arguments with short rebuttals. Time management is extremely important in this speech, as any argument untouched will be “flowed” to the end of the debate and can be counted as a win for the other team since you didn’t touch on it. The best way to rebut an argument is either “null” or “judo”. You can either explain away its relevance (null), rejecting the contention with evidence, or you can flip the argument to make it seem like it actually is an argument in favor of your argument (judo). If you have time after rebutting ALL of your opponents arguments, you can also circle back to reinforce your own. It’s important to acknowledge possible clashes in contentions or frameworks, outlining where your cases differ and why yours is still triumphant. 2nd Rebuttal/Deconstruction (4 mins): Same speech, except the opposing team will now deliver their rebuttal. Similarly to 2nd constructive, speaking 2nd in the debate is advantageous as it gives you time to structure your rebuttals with the addition of now having heard your opponents rebuttal to your own case, giving you the advantage of getting to respond not just to your opponents constructive, but also their rebuttals to your own arguments. 2nd Crossfire (3 mins): The two “2nd speakers” from each team will now enter crossfire. 1st Summary (3 mins): Now, the initial speaker of the entire debate will go back to the podium to deliver their second speech, the summary speech. This is a chance to go over major clashes, revisit frameworks, outline how you win on each major clash, and possibly re-inforce points/rebuttals if necessary. A good way to start off a summary is by acknowledging what occurred in crossfire or may have been left unsaid, as this is fresh in your judges mind. However, try not to linger too much and remember to use your time wisely. Do not use summary as another rebuttal speech—you cannot stay on the defense all the time or it will seem like you’re losing. Think of the summary as a seg-way to the final focus. As the debate progresses, think of a funnel: it begins with technical evidence and logical arguments, and slowly gets more abstract and “big-picture”. 2nd Summary (3 mins): The second speaker will now deliver their summary. Grand Crossfire (3 mins): Now, all four speakers will stand and engage in crossfire. This is where things can get intense, so it's important to remain collected and share your speaking time. This is also a good time to show your judge that both you and your partner have strengths, as if only you or your teammate is speaking, it can look unbalanced. Ensure that your partners are able to have their time to speak, but also that you are getting your fair share. This is where interrupting, arguments, and even yelling can occur, so it's important to speak with a balance of passion and restraint. Try to communicate with your partner (discuss beforehand what method, usually through notes or during prep time) about who will take the first question between the two of you. A harmonic team seems stronger and seeming stronger makes you seem like you have the upper-hand. 1st Final Focus (2 mins): Now, the rebuttalist will go to the podium for their second speech, speaking for the final time on behalf of their team. You are not allowed to introduce new arguments in final focus. Here, you must weigh ultimate impacts. A lot of people develop a framework for their final focuses. Some examples have been “This debate has come down to two worlds” or “This debate has come down to fantasy and reality”. This is a way to segway into how your argument is more realistic, more plausible, has better outcomes, is better long-term, etc. etc. This is where you explain your “big picture” argument, honing in on why your world (the status quo or the resolution) is overall superior. 2nd Final Focus (2 mins): Now, the opposing team will deliver their final speech, and the closing speech of the entire debate. Again, whichever team chose to have second in the debate speaking order will have the advantage of getting the last word with the judge. After the debate, you will shake hands with one another, shake hands with the judge and timer, and politely exit the room. In addition to the speeches, each team gets 3 minutes of prep time to use however they like. This is a silent time to prepare before delivering a speech, to organize thoughts, or to speak with your teammate about your next steps. You can disperse the 3 minutes however you like throughout the debate, but you cannot use it before a crossfire. Most people will save their prep time for final focus and summary speech. Some additional notes:
  • It is rude to make faces/gestures while another person is speaking at the podium
  • It is Alaska debate style to introduce yourself for the first time in the debate, “Hello, my name is …. and me and my partner …. (affirm/negate) the resolution:....”
  • Within your own team, you have a 1st and 2nd speaker. This isn’t to be confused with the 1st and 2nd speaking order of the debate as a whole, as that will differ with each round. You will usually stick with being either 1st or 2nd throughout the tournament. It is up to each team to weigh each person's strengths and weaknesses to decide who will take 1st/2nd.
    1st speaker will deliver the pre-written constructive and the summary speech.
    2nd speaker will deliver the rebuttal and the final focus.
  • It is heavily advised that the more experienced debater take 2nd speaker. Otherwise, whoever is best at impromptu argumentation, knows more evidence, or feels more comfortable being a rebuttalist should take 2nd.
  • After each round, a judge will give you individual “speaker points” (ranging from 20-30). This will vary from you and your partner as you are judged individually for how you performed. This comes down to numerous factors like etiquette, swiftness, composure, and even passion. However, many judges will judge speaker points with different prioritizations and standards, so it's important to care about them, but not place too much importance on it. This is why it's important to stay cordial with your opponents, refrain from speaking over/trumping your partner, and speaking with confidence. It is possible for a team to win on speaker points, but still lose the debate.
  • After each debate, you should debrief and assess your case based on what you learned from each debate.

  • Above all else, have fun :)

Happy debating!
