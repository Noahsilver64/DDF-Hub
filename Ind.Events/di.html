Dramatic Interpretation

(or DI)

By Lilith Rain Bell-Turley

Dramatic Interp Champion, 2025-26

Dramatic Interpretation

Dramatic Interpretation (DI) is a 10 minute forensics event with no props allowed. Throughout the piece you are allowed to change characters, and you are expected to choose a published piece of writing that deals with serious, often emotional topics.


When finding a piece for DI, you need to look for something with heavy topics. DI requires the performer to often grapple with themes of loss, trauma, and heartache. You’ll regularly see pieces that deal with very intense topics such as SA, death, suicide, isolation, and even oppression. More than this though, it should carry a message. Pointless suffering does not win ballots, your character must reflect on their experiences, and despite the hardship should ideally come out of it with something.


Because of this it’s incredibly important that actors handle these topics tastefully. I recommend getting advice from people with similar experiences. While this may be difficult, if possible it can help you tremendously.

This also means making an effort not to “over-act.” You need to ask yourself if you’re accurately encompassing both the ups and downs of these emotions that characters within the piece are experiencing. Sometimes you may need to cry, and scream, but those moments are just as important as moments of happiness, or at least refrain from deep emotional turmoil. These ups and downs will more accurately depict the non-linear reality of these harsh topics. We often sway back and forth between our emotions.


Having eyes on these pieces is key, and you have to be open to criticism – just like in any other event. Getting feedback is the best way to best maintain tact, and find key moments of intensity, and refrain.


Consider this while also doing your blocking. Most pieces will have normal day-to-day moments, and then stark contrasts by using physical movement to convey deep emotional pain. For me, this meant having a moment of calmly handing over clothing items, and separately a moment of desperately grasping at kitchen items to hold myself up. Every piece will necessitate different blocking, but it’s vital you figure out how to add physical aspects, and demeanor shifts, to sell the feelings and experiences you are trying to communicate.

Ultimately you need to really get into the head of your character. When were they once happy? As silly as it may feel you have to dig into your motivations, or even what your motivations once were.

You may need to recut your piece over and over again using all of this. I myself didn’t finalize my ending till before state. Taking that time to dig into every little detail of the work can make or break a ballot for you. Every second counts, and that includes moments of silence and reflection. In a way you need to cut it like a debate case, every word needs to have impact, or contribute to the themes and message you’re trying to convey.


Part of your cutting will be other characters. Unlike other pieces, character shifts need to be much more solemn. My sister and I both used snaps for our pieces, but they often had much softer disposition shifts for each character. Character changes can also be seen as an interruption, that’s why these soft, seamless transitions are so important, and why you should try to avoid too many character shifts. With that being said, I believe having more than one character can really make your piece. Many people have amazing pieces with just one character, but properly integrating another character or even a few, can set your piece above the rest.

Aside from the written piece of work, you’re going to need an introduction. I personally start by introducing the themes of the story (E.G. “the loss of a child is an inconsolable anguish…”). If it feels appropriate, you could even add a quote to help really sell the pains and emotional turbulence the characters you depict are experiencing. From here you have to transition into a brief explanation of what is occurring in the story, ideally by tying in the primary character to the struggle you’ve explained. Your goal is to briefly introduce the themes, plot, and emotional impact of your story.


What makes DI such a compelling event is its deep connection to humanity, and how we function as people. It fosters empathy, and allows a forum to share about struggles we don’t talk about day to day. It can both be a heavy burden, and deeply cathartic. Mistreating a topic is something you have to be extremely mindful of, but don’t let that deter you from trying to act out a piece you’re passionate about.

If you develop the emotional capacity and understanding, the only thing standing in your way will be technical aspects. I always recommend asking captains and peers for example work, and feedback. Don’t stop pushing to make it as strong as it can be, because DI is one of the events with an incredible potential to really touch an audience.
